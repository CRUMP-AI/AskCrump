Fact-check AI résumé bullets before they reach an application.

A polished bullet can still be wrong. Test the verb, scope, metric, causal claim, and interview story while the résumé is still an editable draft.

Take this line from Ask Crump’s fictional product-operations résumé sample:

Built launch-readiness reviews across product, sales, support, and marketing, improving on-time enablement from 68% to 92%.

It reads cleanly. It also makes several factual claims: the person built the reviews, four functions used them, “on-time” had a definition, performance began at 68%, performance ended at 92%, and the change was connected to the person’s work. Every claim needs support.

That is a useful role for AI in résumé work. It can help organize and sharpen what happened. It cannot turn a plausible sentence into a true one.

Why the sample is useful

Its bullets are specific enough to expose the exact facts a real applicant would need to verify.

The five questions behind a defensible bullet

  1. What did you personally do?

    Underline the action you can own. “Built,” “led,” and “created” imply responsibility. If you contributed to work owned by someone else, “supported,” “partnered on,” or “contributed to” may be more accurate and can still be valuable.

  2. What was the real scope?

    Check every team, location, product, customer group, and time period. “Across four functions” is different from “with representatives from four functions.” “Company-wide” is not a harmless synonym for “used by my team.”

  3. Can you reproduce the number?

    Keep the source, date range, definition, numerator, denominator, and calculation. If you cannot explain why a metric changed from 68% to 92%, do not present it as settled fact.

  4. Does the sentence overstate cause?

    “Improved” often reads as “my work caused this result.” If several changes happened at once, describe the work and observed result separately until the causal claim is supportable.

  5. Could you explain it without the résumé?

    An interviewer may ask what changed, who disagreed, how you measured the result, and what you would do differently. If the bullet falls apart after two follow-up questions, revise it before applying.

“Returned approximately 12 analyst hours per week”Time was measured or estimated consistently and the saved work went somewhere useful.Before-and-after task sample, analyst count, calculation method, observation period, and downstream use.“Automated recurring reports and reduced weekly manual reporting work.”
“Helped reduce average resolution time by 18%”The reduction is real, the time window is comparable, and the contribution is bounded.Issue cohort, start and end definitions, baseline, comparison window, confounders, and team contribution.“Mapped order-exception workflows and supported a resolution-time improvement initiative.”

The safer versions are fallbacks, not automatic improvements. If the evidence exists, keep the specific claim and retain the records that explain it.

A prompt that keeps the facts in charge

Before using Ask Crump—or any writing tool—assemble a small evidence bank. Use only material you are allowed to share.

I am applying for [role]. Below are facts from my actual work and the target job description.

For each proposed résumé bullet:
1. use only facts present in my evidence bank;
2. label any unsupported claim, number, scope, title, tool, or causal statement;
3. separate what I did from what the team did;
4. preserve dates, units, and definitions exactly;
5. give me a specific version and a conservative version;
6. list the evidence I should verify before applying.

Do not invent metrics, employers, credentials, tools, responsibilities, or outcomes. If a detail is missing, ask me for it instead of filling the gap.

Evidence bank:
[paste accurate facts]

Target role:
[paste only material you are allowed to use]

The prompt is deliberately less glamorous than “make my résumé amazing.” It gives the tool a useful job: make the evidence easier to understand without quietly changing it.

Review the file, not just the paragraph

Ask Crump can package a résumé as an editable Word document. The final review still belongs in the file you will submit.

  • Open the .docx in the software you will use to deliver it.
  • Confirm your name, contact details, links, employers, titles, dates, education, and credentials.
  • Compare every number with its source and check that verb tense matches the employment dates.
  • Remove confidential or employer-owned details you are not authorized to disclose.
  • Use one or two pages only when that length suits the role—not because a template forced it.
  • Read the résumé aloud and prepare a short explanation for every bullet.
  • Export to PDF only if the application requests or accepts it, then inspect that final file separately.
Human-review limit

Readable headings and restrained formatting can help, but no layout guarantees parsing by every applicant tracking system. No wording guarantees ranking, recruiter approval, an interview, or a job.
